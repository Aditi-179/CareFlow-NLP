# CLINICAL REFERENCE: SYSTEMIC HYPERTENSION

## 1. Overview and Definitions
Hypertension (high blood pressure) is a widespread cardiovascular condition defined as persistent elevation of systemic arterial pressure. It is often referred to as the "silent killer" because it typically produces no discernible symptoms until significant end-organ damage has occurred to the brain, heart, kidneys, and vasculature.

## 2. Clinical Classification Stages (ACC/AHA Guidelines)
- Normal Blood Pressure: Systolic < 120 mm Hg AND Diastolic < 80 mm Hg.
- Elevated Blood Pressure: Systolic 120–129 mm Hg AND Diastolic < 80 mm Hg.
- Stage 1 Hypertension: Systolic 130–139 mm Hg OR Diastolic 80–89 mm Hg.
- Stage 2 Hypertension: Systolic ≥ 140 mm Hg OR Diastolic ≥ 90 mm Hg.
- Hypertensive Crisis: Systolic > 180 mm Hg and/or Diastolic > 120 mm Hg. When accompanied by acute target-organ damage (e.g., chest pain, shortness of breath, neurological deficits, visual changes), this constitutes a medical emergency requiring immediate hospitalization.

## 3. Etiology and Risk Factors
- Primary (Essential) Hypertension: Accounts for 90–95% of cases with no identifiable single secondary cause. It develops multifactorially from interacting genetic predispositions, advanced age, high dietary sodium intake (>2,300 mg/day), low potassium intake, sedentary lifestyle, visceral obesity, excessive alcohol consumption, and chronic sympathetic nervous activation.
- Secondary Hypertension: Resulting from an underlying identifiable condition such as chronic kidney disease (CKD), renal artery stenosis, primary aldosteronism, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), Cushing syndrome, or endocrine/vascular disorders.

## 4. Evidence-Based Lifestyle Modifications
Non-pharmacological interventions are fundamental to both hypertension prevention and management:
- Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) Diet: Rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, poultry, fish, and low-fat dairy products, while sharply restricting saturated fats and sweets. DASH can reduce systolic blood pressure by 8 to 14 mm Hg.
- Dietary Sodium Restriction: Lowering sodium consumption to ideally < 1,500 mg per day (or at least a reduction of 1,000 mg/day) produces significant blood pressure reduction.
- Potassium Supplementation/Intake: Increasing dietary potassium (3,500–5,000 mg/day) through whole foods such as leafy greens, bananas, avocados, and legumes helps counteract sodium effects.
- Aerobic and Resistance Exercise: 90 to 150 minutes per week of dynamic aerobic exercise (walking, cycling, swimming) combined with dynamic isometric/resistance sessions reduces systolic BP by 5 to 8 mm Hg.
- Weight Reduction: Achieving and maintaining normal body mass index (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m²). For every 1 kg of body weight lost, systolic blood pressure typically decreases by approximately 1 mm Hg.
- Alcohol Moderation: Limiting consumption to ≤ 2 standard drinks daily for men and ≤ 1 drink daily for women.
- Stress Management and Smoking Cessation: Tobacco causes immediate acute vasoconstriction and chronic endothelial injury.

## 5. Pharmacotherapy Overview
When blood pressure remains uncontrolled despite lifestyle interventions, major first-line antihypertensive drug classes include:
- Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme (ACE) Inhibitors (e.g., Lisinopril, Enalapril).
- Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs) (e.g., Losartan, Valsartan).
- Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs) (e.g., Amlodipine, Diltiazem).
- Thiazide-type Diuretics (e.g., Chlorthalidone, Hydrochlorothiazide).
Treatment regimens are individualized according to comorbidities such as diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or coronary artery disease.
